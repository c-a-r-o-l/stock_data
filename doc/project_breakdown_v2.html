Quant / ML portfolio project · reference map

A market-state retrieval and prediction system

Every part of this project is one of two things: a step you can't skip, or a fork where you pick a path. This document lays out both — the steps, the forks, the option chosen at each fork, and why. It also marks the concepts worth learning as you go.

Strip away the vocabulary and the whole system is k-nearest-neighbors on vectors: turn each historical market window into a list of numbers, store them, and when today arrives find the closest past windows and look at what happened next. Everything sophisticated — encoders, FAISS, embeddings — is just an argument about where the vectors come from and whether the complexity earns its place. The project's real subject is that argument.
Operating principle Rigor beats architecture. The thing that makes a quant interviewer take this seriously is not the fanciest model — it is visible, ruthless skepticism about your own results. Build the simple version first; force every complex addition to prove it beats the simple one on data it has never seen. A project that says "I built the deep model, tested it honestly, and it didn't justify its cost" is stronger than one claiming a suspicious Sharpe of 2.5.

00

Framing & the real question

Before any code, decide what question the project answers. The framing is itself the most valuable design choice.

Don't frame this as "I built a stock predictor." Frame it as a research hypothesis: does a learned sequence representation beat hand-built factors for medium-horizon equity prediction? That single reframing converts a show-and-tell into a controlled experiment — which is exactly what real quant research is: form a hypothesis, test it without fooling yourself, report what's true even when it's disappointing.

The trap this avoids The "ooo I used ML" project. Bolting a Transformer onto a problem that hand-built features already solve is the single most common way these projects ring hollow. If you frame the project as a comparison, a negative result ("the encoder didn't help") becomes a finding rather than a failure — and demonstrated self-skepticism is the trait quant desks screen hardest for.

Everything below serves that comparison. The pipeline produces predictions two ways — a simple way and a sophisticated way — and the project's spine is measuring whether the sophistication was worth it.

Architecture decision — locked The unit of analysis is the (stock, date) cross-sectional factor vector, standardized against that day's peers. The alternative — per-stock time-sequences collapsed to one embedding per stock — is rejected because the label is cross-sectional (top 10% of stocks that day) and a representation that never saw the peers cannot reconstruct a cross-sectional rank. This is not a preference; it's a structural mismatch. The per-stock sequence architecture is the root cause of nearly every other problem in Plan 2, so the merge adopts the cross-sectional design wholesale.
01

Data foundation

The least glamorous section and the one most likely to silently invalidate everything else. Get this wrong and every downstream number is fiction.

Build a point-in-time universe Necessary

Your "200 stocks" must mean which 200 were in your universe on each historical date — including ones that later went bankrupt or were delisted — not "200 companies that exist today."

Why it matters: if you pick today's survivors, you've excluded every company that died, and survivors went up by definition. This is survivorship bias, and it produces a gorgeous backtest that means nothing. You need point-in-time data with historical universe construction.

Adjust prices correctly Necessary

Handle corporate actions — splits, dividends, spinoffs. A 2-for-1 split halves the raw price overnight; if unadjusted, your model sees a fake −50% return.

Why it matters: unadjusted data injects enormous phantom moves that have nothing to do with anything tradeable.

Guarantee no future information leaks Necessary

Every feature computed for date t may use only data available at the close of t. Nothing from t+1 onward.

Why it matters: look-ahead bias (a form of data leakage) is the cardinal sin of backtesting. It's so easy to commit accidentally that you should write a test that actively hunts for it (see §09).

Align multi-frequency data to a daily calendar Necessary Craft

Data arrives at different frequencies — daily (OHLC, VIX), weekly (MACD, jobless claims), monthly (CPI), quarterly (EPS, revenue). Every day in your dataset must have the same number of columns, so slower frequencies are forward-filled to daily: the last known value is carried forward until the next release. This is exactly how a real-time system works.

Why it matters: a unified daily grid is the prerequisite for every downstream operation — vector construction, normalization, retrieval. The forward-fill plumbing is reusable across the entire pipeline, and Plan 2's data-alignment section gets this right.

Align macro data to release dates, not period dates Differentiator

GDP for Q1 (Jan–Mar) is not available on April 1 — it's published at the end of April. Align every macro series to its release date, not to the start or end of the measurement period. This is release-date alignment.

Why it matters: aligning Q1 GDP to January 1 injects three months of future information and produces a backtest that looks amazing and means nothing. This is the single most common look-ahead in macro-aware quant projects — and Plan 2 explicitly gets it right, so it carries forward into the merge.

Make forward-fill staleness visible, not trusted Differentiator

Quarterly features forward-filled to daily are constant ~98% of the time. In a distance metric a stale constant can dominate "closeness" or be over-represented by column count. Don't trust the encoder or the distance function to handle this silently.

Fix: check each factor's constancy/variance share; down-weight or drop near-constant features in the distance calculation; optionally add a "days-since-last-update" feature per factor so staleness is modeled explicitly. This is inspectable in Vector A; a learned embedding hides it — another reason the hand-built vector is the core.
02

Signals (feature engineering)

The "Level 1" of the original design. For each stock each day, compute the numbers that describe its state. This is where domain knowledge lives.

Choose your factors Necessary

Design a set of factors grounded in factor investing — momentum (relative strength), trend (distance above a moving average), value (valuation ratios), quality, and fundamentals (EPS revisions), plus volatility. This is feature engineering.

Why this is the right starting representation: finance has decades of knowledge about what carries signal. The fact that you can write these features down by hand is exactly why an encoder (§04) has so little to add here — and why hand-built features are the baseline everything must beat.

Normalize before anything compares them Necessary

Put factors on a comparable scale — standardization (z-score to mean 0, standard deviation 1), typically cross-sectional (rank each stock against the others that day). Also handle outliers via winsorization.

Silent killer Nearest-neighbor search measures "closeness." If one factor ranges 0–1 and another ranges 0–10,000, the big-scale factor dominates the distance and drowns out the rest — your neighbors become garbage, chosen by whichever number happens to be largest. Skip normalization and the whole retrieval layer quietly breaks.

Decide whether to discretize Optional bet

Decision · keep factors continuous, or bin them into tokens?
Keep continuous vectors Lean here
A day is a vector of real-valued factors. Simpler, loses no information, feeds retrieval directly.
Discretize into tokens Only if needed
Discretization / binning (e.g. RS_90=95.4 → [RS_90_HIGH]) — the "tokenization" framing from the original plan. Required only if you commit to the Transformer path, which expects token sequences.
Verdict: the language analogy is mostly decorative — within a single day the "tokens" are an unordered bag of flags, not a sentence. Discretizing throws away precision for no inherent gain. Keep factors continuous unless an encoder forces tokens on you.

Don't over-supply signals Differentiator

With ~2,520 days × 200 stocks ≈ 500k rows, your statistical limit is far tighter than your hardware limit. Hardware holds a thousand signals in a few GB; your data can only justify roughly the low tens, maybe up to ~100 if genuinely informative.

Why: too many features on too few rows is an invitation to overfitting — the curse of dimensionality. Knowing the binding constraint is statistical, not computational, is itself a signal of maturity.

Add explicit regime features — don't let standardization decide for you Differentiator

Cross-sectional z-scoring within each day removes the market's absolute level: "+2σ momentum in calm 2017" and "+2σ in the 2020 crash" land at the same coordinate. For a market-state system you've normalized away regime signal you may want neighbors to share. This is not a bug — it's an inherent tradeoff of the standardization you need elsewhere — but it must be handled deliberately rather than accidentally.

Fix: add explicit regime features that are the same for every stock on a given day — VIX / realized vol, index vs 200-day MA, market breadth, cross-sectional dispersion, yield-curve slope, credit spreads. Enter them as raw magnitudes (not binarized). Then decide whether retrieval should match within-regime or across-regime, rather than letting standardization decide by accident. This also feeds the regime-breakdown in §08.
03

Labels (the prediction target)

What is the model actually predicting? Two sub-decisions — the horizon, and how to bucket the outcome.

Set the horizon Necessary

Predict forward returns over ~126 trading days (≈ 6 months), not tomorrow.

Why 126 and not 1: daily returns are almost pure noise — that's high-frequency territory where you have no edge against colocated servers. The factor-style signals you built only pay off over multi-month horizons, where trends and fundamental repricing play out and noise averages down. The horizon that looks weird is actually the soundest decision in the design — it's the only horizon at which your features are coherent.

Choose the label granularity Optional bet

Decision · how many classes? (current plan: top 10% / middle 80% / bottom 10%)
Binary — top decile vs not Try as variant
Easiest to learn, easiest to evaluate (AUC, precision/recall), easiest to explain. A clean detection problem.
Three classes — 10 / 80 / 10 Keep
Already well-designed: it encodes the truth that only the tails carry signal and lumps the indistinguishable middle into one bucket. This is cross-sectional ranking via percentile labeling.
Ten deciles Avoid
Forces the model to separate the 40–50th from the 50–60th percentile — a boundary that is almost entirely noise. Splits scarce data ten ways and invites overfitting.
Continuous rank (regression) Try as variant
Predict the percentile directly (0–1), no arbitrary buckets — classification vs regression as a deliberate test. Harder to train stably.
Verdict: more classes is not better here. Adjacent middle buckets differ only by noise. Treat class count as a hyperparameter — ship 10/80/10, also try binary and continuous-rank, and let held-out performance decide. If you ever want ordered buckets, learn ordinal classification (plain multiclass wastes the ordering).
Subtlety that bites later Because each label looks 126 days ahead, windows near a train/test boundary have labels that bleed across it. Watch for class imbalance too — the tails are rare by construction. The boundary-bleed problem forces the purging step in §07.
04

Representation (the optional encoder)

The most impressive-sounding part and the most skippable part, at the same time. This is the central fork of the whole project.

There are two different vectors in play, and conflating them is the source of most confusion:

  • Vector A — hand-built. Your normalized factors, stacked. Small, interpretable, you know what every slot means. Ready to search today.
  • Vector B — learned. 64–128 numbers that come out of a neural network. They correspond to nothing nameable — slot 47 is not "EPS revision," it's an abstract coordinate training invented. Sized to the problem, not cargo-culted from BERT.
Decision · which vector feeds retrieval?
Vector A — hand-built factors Baseline · build first
No encoder. Interpretable, cheap, immediately searchable. The honest core of the project.
Vector B — learned embeddings Prove it or cut it
Train a Transformer encoder (BERT-style) over the 60-day sequence; take its output as the vector. Requires self-attention, positional encoding, and a training objective.
Verdict: build A first. Treat B as the hypothesis under test, not the foundation.
The label-contamination trap with contrastive learning Training the encoder with contrastive learning on the label (positive pairs = same-outcome windows) bends the representation toward the target before anything predicts the target — so "these historical states are similar to today" partly means "they had similar outcomes," which is what you're trying to predict. Circular. With hand-built Vector A this dissolves by construction — the label touches the pipeline exactly once, at prediction.
If an encoder is tested (gate 6), the constraints are strict Non-label objective only: reconstruction, masked-feature prediction, or next-step — never the forward-return label. 64–128 dim, not 768: sized to ~50 factors and few effective samples, not cargo-culted from BERT's 768. Point-in-time inputs: a self-supervised objective still leaks if the reconstruction target contains forward-looking data. Same pipeline, different vector source: swap Vector B into the identical DB and backtest — that's what makes it a controlled experiment rather than "I also tried a neural net."

Understand why it probably won't help here Differentiator

Encoders earn their keep when the raw input is high-dimensional and impossible to hand-engineer — text, images, audio, order-book ticks. That's representation learning's home turf. Your input is a handful of factors you already chose. You are applying an encoder at the very edge of its useful range, so the burden of proof sits squarely on it.

Where it would earn its place in finance: raw tick / order-book data, news text and earnings-call transcripts, or alt-data like satellite imagery — cases where human feature-engineering breaks down. Knowing encoder vs decoder (why BERT, not GPT) and Sentence Transformers is worth being able to articulate even if you don't ship the encoder.
Honest cost Vector B is a black box. The original plan waves this away by noting the output is still a list of named historical analogues you can eyeball — a fair partial defense — but the embedding itself remains uninterpretable, which for a lot of quant work is disqualifying on its own.
05

Storage & retrieval

Demystified: a "vector database" is a table of number-lists optimized for one operation — given a new list, find the closest stored ones. That's all.

Stand up the index Necessary

Put every historical window's vector (tagged with its forward outcome) into a vector database and search it with FAISS — the engine that makes approximate nearest neighbor search fast at scale (learn its index types, e.g. IVF / HNSW).

Why it's not exotic: a normal database answers "where id = 4" (equality); a vector DB answers "what's nearest this" (closeness). The "database" framing is largely convenience wrapped around k-nearest neighbors. For a personal project, raw FAISS + a file is plenty — you don't need a hosted product.
The key independence The database doesn't care where the vectors come from. Encoder and vector DB are independent choices — you can (and should) run the DB on hand-built Vector A with no encoder at all. Pick your distance metric deliberately (cosine vs Euclidean) since it defines "closeness."
06

Prediction layer

After retrieval returns the nearest windows, turn them into a forecast. The tools have native jobs — don't make one do another's.

Clear up the tool confusion FAISS finds instances; XGBoost predicts outcomes. FAISS already ranks neighbors by closeness, so you never need a model to "find the closest one." And XGBoost is not a search engine — it doesn't return rows from your database. They are not parallel paths — XGBoost's stage-1 features come from the FAISS neighborhood. Keep each in its lane.

Build the simple predictor first Baseline

Distance-weighted k-NN regression: take the ~100 nearest windows, weight closer ones more, average their forward outcomes. Interpretable, fast, and the bar everything else must clear.

Then the two-stage upgrade Optional bet

Stage 1 — summarize the neighborhood into features. Don't just average outcomes; compute descriptive numbers about the 100 neighbors: mean outcome, spread (did they agree or disagree?), how close they actually were (dense cluster vs lone window with distant matches), fraction positive. "100 neighbors that strongly agreed" is a different signal from "100 scattered neighbors that disagreed."

Stage 2 — feed those neighborhood features plus today's static factors into gradient boosting (XGBoost / LightGBM). Now the model does its native job: learning the nonlinear mapping to a calibrated probability, capturing interactions like "agreement matters, but only when volatility is low."

Why this beats a plain average: the average throws away everything but the mean — it can't tell a confident neighborhood from a confused one. The two-stage version feeds the quality of the match into the final call.
Order: ship weighted k-NN first; keep the XGBoost layer only if it beats k-NN on held-out data. The comparison is the project. Concepts to know for credibility: probability calibration, ensembling / stacking, and feature importance / SHAP for the boosting layer.
Neighbor-pool leakage — a second purge, separate from the train/test split Stage-1 features summarize neighbors' forward outcomes. If a neighbor's label period extends past the query date t, that feature leaks post-t info. Standard train/test purging does NOT catch this — the leak is at query construction, not at the split boundary. Admissibility rule: a historical point may enter the neighbor set for query t only if its entire label period ended strictly before t. With a 126-day horizon, the neighbor must be dated ≥ 126 days before t. Enforce this at every retrieval. Purge in two places: the split AND the neighbor pool.

There's one genuine alternative: skip explicit retrieval and let the network output the probability directly (the classifier head from §04). More accurate in principle, but you lose the "here are the 100 analogues" story — which is exactly what makes a retrieval system explainable to a PM and easy to talk about in an interview. For your purposes the retrieval version is more defensible because you can show your work.

07

Validation & preventing overfitting

Where amateur quant projects quietly die. The question "how can you test on historical data?" is healthy — the answer is: never train on all of it.

Split by time, never randomly Necessary

Train on the earliest dates, validate on a later slice, test on the latest — and never let the model touch the test slice during training. Use walk-forward / time-series cross-validation, not a random shuffle.

Why not shuffle: a random 80/20 split puts future dates into training and past dates into test — the future leaks into the past. In normal ML you can shuffle; in markets every sample is dated and ordering is information.

Purge and embargo around the label window Differentiator

Leave a 126-day gap between train and test so a window's forward-looking label can't bleed across the boundary. This is purging and embargoing.

Why: without the gap, training data literally contains information about the test period and your backtest is a leak dressed as a result. This is rare in student work and exactly what an interviewer probes.

Report against effective N, not raw window count Differentiator

Overlapping windows with long forward-return labels mean thousands of "samples" contain only a few hundred independent observations. The embargo protects the train/test boundary but not independence within a set — adjacent windows share most of their label period, so metrics inflate from sample re-use, not from signal.

The honest approach — train on everything, report on effective N: keep overlapping windows for training (more samples still help fitting), but attach every headline metric to its true independent-sample count: effective N ≈ calendar span ÷ label horizon (e.g. "AUC 0.71, effective N ≈ 20 periods/stock, not 8,000 windows"). Measure test metrics only on held-out outcomes that share no label period with training. Bootstrap confidence intervals over non-overlapping blocks, not individual days — and combine with the deflated Sharpe for the number of variants tried. Report honest CIs: "Sharpe 0.8, 95% CI [0.2, 1.4] on ~20 independent periods."

Respect the multiple-testing problem Differentiator

The silent killer almost nobody admits to Try twenty strategy variants, keep the best, and you've overfit to your own backtest by hand — you used the test set as a slow training set. This is multiple testing / data snooping. The brutal fix: iterate only on the validation slice, and touch the true test slice exactly once, at the very end. Learn the deflated Sharpe ratio to quantify the inflation.

Apply standard regularization Craft

The model-level tools — regularization (dropout, weight decay), early stopping — matter but are table stakes; they're not where projects fail. Understand the bias-variance tradeoff underneath them.

08

Backtesting

A simulation that replays history as if trading live, using only information that existed at each moment. A clean backtest can still lie — but a dirty one always does.

Simulate with realistic frictions Necessary

Walk forward through the held-out period; at each date act only on information available then; when a signal fires, simulate the trade and hold 126 days. Subtract transaction costs — bid-ask spread, commissions, slippage.

Why costs are non-negotiable: many strategies that look profitable gross are negative net of costs. "Did you include costs?" is an opening question; "no" ends the conversation. Show how Sharpe degrades as costs rise.

Report risk-adjusted metrics, not just return Differentiator

Compute the equity curve and then Sharpe ratio, Sortino (downside-only volatility), Calmar, maximum drawdown, hit rate, and turnover.

Why: "22% annualized" sounds naive; "Sharpe 0.9, max drawdown 18%, high turnover so cost-sensitive" sounds like someone who's sat on a desk. Risk-adjusted thinking is the entire language of the field.

Beat the dumb baselines Necessary

Compare against benchmarks: buy-and-hold the index, a moving-average crossover, logistic regression on raw factors.

Why: the project's entire claim is "the sophisticated thing beats the dumb thing." Without the comparison, no number means anything. If you can't beat "buy the index and nap," there's no value — and saying that honestly is a strength.

Break results down by regime Differentiator

Show performance across regimes — bull vs bear, high- vs low-volatility, stress periods (2020, 2022).

Why: strategies aren't uniformly good; a serious quant knows when their edge exists. Even returns bucketed by VIX level shows this.

Ask whether the result is luck Differentiator

Is your Sharpe distinguishable from chance? Use bootstrap confidence intervals over non-overlapping blocks (not individual days — they share label periods and inflate the effective sample), and combine with the deflated Sharpe from §07.

Why: a single history is one sample; a good-looking backtest can be pure noise. Demonstrating you know this is a genuinely advanced signal. Note the residual humility: the past is one sample, so even a clean backtest is fallible — out-of-sample paper-trading is the only real proof.
09

Engineering & craft

Says "I can ship, not just notebook." This directly answers the feedback that vibe-coded projects fail because you can't speak to the code. Here, be able to speak to every line.

Make it modular and reproducible Craft

Separate modules for data, features, model, backtest. A config file instead of hardcoded values. A fixed seed so results reproduce. A README that lets someone run it.

Why: modular pipeline design and reproducibility (seeds, config) are the contrast between "engineered" and "vibe-coded" — exactly what an interviewer probes when they ask you to walk through the repo.

Version control with intent Craft

Meaningful commits in git, and ideally light experiment tracking — even a logged table of config → out-of-sample Sharpe across your runs.

Why: signals you treat ML as disciplined empirical practice, not a pile of one-off scripts.

Write the leakage test Differentiator

Even a few unit tests — and the most quant-relevant one asserts that no feature on date t uses data from t+1.

Why: look-ahead is the cardinal sin, so a test that actively hunts for it is a flex. It proves you understand the failure mode well enough to automate catching it.

Make the pipeline reasonably fast Craft

A vectorized pipeline; show you can profile and fix a bottleneck (performance profiling).

Why: especially for US / Chicago desks, this connects to the C++/performance dimension they care about.
10

Narrative & writeup

Underrated, often decisive. The difference between a school project and a piece of research is the framing around it.

Write it up as a question, not a product Differentiator

Methodology, results, and an honest conclusion — including a negative result if that's what you got. "Does a learned representation beat hand-built factors at this horizon?" with a real answer.

Why: this is what quant research looks like. A demonstrable web app or clean repo plus this writeup turns the project into your interview "main narrative" — the thing you can talk about for fifteen minutes.

Name your own limitations first Differentiator

A section on what would break this in production: capacity constraints, regime change, the fact that you used daily closes you can't actually trade at.

Why: naming your project's weaknesses before the interviewer does is one of the strongest possible signals — it's precisely the self-skepticism the field runs on.
What NOT to add Resist piling on more models and data sources. The amateur failure mode is breadth without depth — five half-built models, none evaluated. One strategy, evaluated to death with everything above, is far stronger. This is the same scope lesson from the "our journey" app: finished, rigorous, and modest beats sprawling and unfinished, every time.

The one thing to hold onto

The version of this project that helps your applications most is almost the opposite of the version that sounds most impressive. Plan 2 sells maximal architecture — Transformer encoders, contrastive learning, 768-dim embeddings. What Jane Street, QRT, and GSA actually screen for is maximal rigor — the baselines, the leakage controls, the cost modeling, the willingness to report that the cool model lost.

The merge resolves the conflict by adopting the breakdown's cross-sectional (stock, date) spine and treating Plan 2 as reusable data plumbing plus a single late-stage experiment. The encoder is not a co-equal path — it's a challenger that must beat hand-built Vector A on unseen data, using a non-label objective and dimensions sized to the problem (64–128, not 768). That sequencing — build A, freeze the pipeline, swap B in, re-run the identical backtest — is what turns "I have two methods" into "I ran a controlled experiment." Which, per this document's own thesis, is the project.
